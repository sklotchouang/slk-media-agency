Most lists of podcast interview questions sort them by category: icebreakers, personal, career, closing. That helps when you know nothing about the guest. It helps less when the guest is a founder on Tuesday and a novelist on Thursday. A founder has a company, a number and a hard decision behind them. An author has a book, an argument and a reader. The best questions for each are not the same. So this list is sorted by guest type. You get 10 openers, 70 questions split across five kinds of guest, 10 follow-up questions and 10 closing questions. That is 100, numbered 1 to 100 with no gaps. If you're just starting out and preparing for your first guest, take the openers, five questions for that guest type and two closers. Before the list, we compare what three question guides say about preparation, with the date each one shows. We read all three on 27 November 2026.

What makes a good podcast interview question?

A good question asks for something only this guest can give you: a story, a decision, a reason. It is open-ended, it asks one thing, and it is short enough to hold in your head while the guest answers. The best questions come from good preparation and real curiosity, not from a longer list of questions.

Buzzsprout puts the first rule simply in its guide of 6 January 2026: "If you ask yes/no questions, you're going to get yes/no answers." Riverside's guide, last updated 23 July 2026, gives an example. "Was that difficult?" stalls the conversation. Asking what made it difficult gets the story. Riverside also warns against asking more than one question at a time, because the guest then has to decide which part to answer.

The second rule is to listen. Your prepared list gets the guest talking. Your follow-up questions are what the listener remembers. There is research behind this. In 2017, the researchers Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson and Gino published a study of live conversations in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. They found that "people who ask more questions, particularly follow-up questions, are better liked by their conversation partners." They also found that people "do not anticipate that question-asking increases interpersonal liking." The study looked at get-to-know-you chats and speed dates, not podcasts. Still, a guest who likes you relaxes, and a relaxed guest tells better stories.

How many questions should you prepare for a podcast interview?

Riverside's six to ten for a 30-minute interview is a sound base. We work from a simple rule on top of it: prepare about one question for every five minutes of the episode you want to publish, then pick three of them that must be asked whatever happens. A 45-minute episode gets about nine. The other questions are a reserve for when an answer runs short.

Buzzsprout makes the same point from the other side: you do not need to ask every question. A good answer that runs ten minutes is worth more than three short answers that tick boxes. The list is a map. The guest decides the route.

Pick the three must-ask questions from what the episode title depends on. If the episode is about how a founder set their first price, the pricing question is one of the three, and it is the one question you cannot skip. Ask your guest the other questions only if time allows. Podcasters who record solo episodes can use the same list differently: pick a question as a prompt, answer it yourself, and you have an outline.

Should you send podcast interview questions to guests in advance?

Send the topics, not the script. Both 2026 guides agree on this. Buzzsprout says "There's no requirement to send your guest a list of questions before the episode", and suggests sharing the topics you plan to cover instead. Riverside says the same, with two exceptions: send the full list when the guest asks for it, or when the topic is sensitive or full of facts the guest may need to check.

The reason is simple. A guest who has read your exact questions often prepares exact answers, and prepared answers sound recited. A guest who knows the topics comes ready with the facts and still answers in their own words.

In what order should you ask the questions?

Start easy, build trust, then ask the hard ones. Riverside advises against opening with questions about the guest's personal life. Open on their work today, which they can answer without thinking. Then ask for a story, then the method, then the tension: the mistake, the disagreement, the view their peers would argue with. Close with one idea the listener can carry away, and where to find the guest.

That order covers the four types of questions every interview needs. Icebreaker questions build rapport and give the guest a minute to settle before the real conversation. Background questions establish the guest's credibility with your audience, so keep them short: one question about the guest's background is enough, because the intro already did most of that work. Story and expertise questions carry the episode. Closing questions give the listener a next step. Each type has a job, and a guest who gets all four in that order tends to give thoughtful responses rather than rehearsed ones.

The 100 questions below follow that order inside each group. Here is how the list is laid out.

Questions to open any interview (1 to 10)

Icebreaker questions should be easy to answer and point toward the topic of the episode. Pick one, two at most.

  1. What are you working on this week that has your full attention?
  2. How do you explain what you do to someone you just met?
  3. What did your work look like a year ago, compared with today?
  4. What is a question people ask you all the time, and what is your short answer?
  5. What got you interested in this topic in the first place?
  6. What is one thing about your work that would surprise our listeners?
  7. Where are you recording from today, and what does a normal day there look like?
  8. What was the last thing you changed your mind about in your field?
  9. If a listener only remembers one thing from today, what should it be?
  10. What made you say yes to this conversation?

Business podcast interview questions for founders and business owners (11 to 30)

Founders have numbers, decisions and people behind every answer. Ask for the specific moment, not the summary. These business podcast interview questions work for the owner of a small firm as well as a venture-backed CEO. On a B2B podcast, where the target audience is other buyers and operators, the questions about pricing, hiring and channels tend to get the most useful answers.

  1. What problem did you see before you started the company?
  2. Who was your first paying customer, and how did you find them?
  3. What did you sell before you had the product you sell today?
  4. What was the moment you knew the business would work?
  5. What was the moment you thought it would not?
  6. Which early decision would you reverse if you could?
  7. How did you set your first price, and how has it changed?
  8. What did your first hire do, and why that role first?
  9. What is a task you still do yourself that most founders hand off?
  10. What is a task you handed off too late?
  11. Which customer complaint changed your product the most?
  12. What does your week look like now, compared with the first year?
  13. How do you decide what not to build?
  14. What is the hardest conversation you have had with a cofounder or partner?
  15. What did you get wrong about your market at the start?
  16. Which channel brings you the best customers, and which looked good but did not?
  17. What advice do founders in your space get that you think is wrong?
  18. How do you know when to keep going with an idea and when to stop?
  19. What would you tell an owner who is exactly where you were three years ago?
  20. What does the next stage of the business need from you that the last one did not?

Questions for authors (31 to 45)

An author has already told the story once, in print. Your job is to get the parts that did not make the book, and the argument behind it.

  1. What argument does the book make, in one sentence?
  2. Who did you write it for, and how do you picture that reader?
  3. What made you sure this needed to be a book and not an article?
  4. What did you cut from the final draft that you still think about?
  5. Which chapter was the hardest to write, and why?
  6. What did your research turn up that changed your view?
  7. Who would disagree with the book most strongly, and what would they say?
  8. What is a story from the book that you tell most often?
  9. What is a story that did not make it in?
  10. What has a reader told you that you did not expect?
  11. How did you work out the title?
  12. What does your writing day actually look like?
  13. What did you learn about your topic after the book came out?
  14. If a listener reads only one chapter, which should it be?
  15. What are you writing next, and how does it connect to this one?

Questions for experts and specialists (46 to 60)

An expert in their field can talk for an hour without saying anything a listener can use. Pull them toward real cases and plain words, and ask them to share their expertise without a lecture.

  1. How would you explain your field to a twelve year old?
  2. What is the most common mistake people make in your area?
  3. What is a myth about your field that will not go away?
  4. Walk me through a real case you worked on, from the first call to the result.
  5. What did the client or patient believe at the start that turned out to be wrong?
  6. What is a sign that someone needs help from a person like you?
  7. What can a listener do on their own, and where should they stop and call an expert?
  8. Which tools or methods do you use that most people in your field do not?
  9. What has changed in your field in the last two years?
  10. Where do you disagree with others in your field?
  11. What question do you wish people asked you more often?
  12. What does a beginner get right that experienced people forget?
  13. What is a term from your field that listeners should know, and what does it mean?
  14. Which result from your work are you proudest of, and what made it possible?
  15. What would you check first if you were in the listener's position today?

Questions for coaches and consultants (61 to 70)

Coaches and consultants sell a method. Ask them to show the method working on one real person, with the details changed if they need to protect the client.

  1. What does a client usually say in the very first call?
  2. What is the real problem behind what they say?
  3. Describe a client you helped, without names. Where did they start and where did they end?
  4. What is the first thing you ask a new client to stop doing?
  5. Which part of your method do clients resist the most?
  6. What did you change in your method after it failed with a client?
  7. When do you tell someone you are not the right fit?
  8. What can a listener try this week from your method, with no coach in the room?
  9. How do you know a client has made real progress, and not just felt better?
  10. What advice in your space do you think does harm?

Questions for creators and fellow podcasters (71 to 80)

A creator or another host knows the craft of your medium. These questions work well for podcast guests who run their own show or channel, and they give your listeners a look behind the microphone. They fit a video podcast as well as an audio show, and a marketer or a YouTube creator can answer them as easily as another host.

  1. What was the first piece of content that made people notice you?
  2. What did you make before that, which nobody saw?
  3. How do you pick what to make next?
  4. What does your process look like from idea to publish?
  5. Which piece did you expect to do well that did not, and why do you think it failed?
  6. How has your audience changed since you started?
  7. What is a comment or message from your audience that changed what you make?
  8. How do you handle the weeks when you do not want to make anything?
  9. Which platform has been the best fit for you, and which was a waste of time?
  10. What would you tell someone about to publish their first episode or video?

Follow-up questions that work with any guest (81 to 90)

Follow-up questions are where the interview gets good. Keep these ten in your head, not on your page, and use them when an answer is close to a story but not there yet.

  1. What happened next?
  2. What did that look like in practice?
  3. Can you give me an example?
  4. How did you feel when that happened?
  5. Why do you think that worked?
  6. What would you do differently now?
  7. Who else was in the room?
  8. What did you say to them?
  9. What did that cost you?
  10. You said something a minute ago that I want to go back to. Can you say more about it?

Closing questions (91 to 100)

Closing questions should leave the listener with one idea and one next step. Ask one or two, then thank the guest and stop. Decide which closer you will use before you hit record, so the end of the episode does not trail off.

  1. What is one thing a listener can do this week based on today?
  2. Is there a question you wish I had asked you?
  3. What are you curious about right now that you have not figured out?
  4. Who should I talk to next on this topic?
  5. What is one book, show or person that shaped how you think about this?
  6. What would you tell yourself on the first day you started?
  7. What do you hope listeners take away from this conversation?
  8. What are you working on that you want people to know about?
  9. Where is the best place for listeners to find you?
  10. Is there anything we talked about that you want to correct or add?

Which podcast interview questions should you avoid asking?

The guides agree on a short list. Riverside names three: personal life questions to open the interview, yes or no questions, and more than one question at a time. Add three of our own:

  • Questions the guest's bio already answers. "So tell us about yourself" uses up the first five minutes on facts the listener could read in the show notes.
  • Questions that contain the answer. "Isn't it true that consistency matters most?" gives the guest nothing to do but agree.
  • Questions every other host asks. If the guest has done a podcast tour, they have answered "what does your morning routine look like?" many times already. Read or listen to one of their recent interviews and ask something it did not cover.

Which questions give you the best clips?

A clip needs a complete thought in under a minute: a claim, a reason, and an example or a turn. Questions that ask for a moment ("What was the moment you thought it would not work?"), a disagreement ("Where do you disagree with others in your field?") or a mistake ("Which early decision would you reverse?") tend to produce that shape. Questions that ask for a list or a summary rarely do. When you edit, look for the answers that start with a scene. If you use software to find clips, check the moments it picks against that shape. How do you write your own questions for a specific guest?

Use this list as a starting point, then make it specific in four steps:

  1. Read or listen to one recent interview with the guest. Note the questions they have already answered well, and skip them.
  2. Find one fact only research would turn up. A line from their book, a post they wrote, a product they shut down. Build one question around it. It shows the guest you did the work, and it gets an answer they have not given before.
  3. Pick the guest type above and take five questions. Rewrite each one with the guest's own names, numbers and places. "What was your hardest hire?" becomes "Why did you hire a head of sales before a second engineer?" A specific challenge gets a specific answer.
  4. Add one opener, two closers and the follow-ups. Mark the three questions that must be asked.

Do not rehearse the questions word for word. Know the guest's story well enough that you can ask the next question in your own words. That is what makes the process repeatable from one episode to the next: the list of questions changes, the method does not.

The listener should be the test for every question. Before you keep one, ask: would someone who has never heard of this guest want to hear the answer?
